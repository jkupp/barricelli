Numerical evolution — after N. A. Barricelli, 1953–57

Barricelli's Universe

In 1953, on the IAS computer at Princeton, Nils Aall Barricelli seeded a strip of numbers and watched them reproduce, cross, parasitize and mutate. Instead of using a "fitness function" analogous to natural selection, he programmed his universe so that numbers could come together and help each other survive. Each row below is one generation.

How one generation is built

See how an existing generation gives rise to a new generation in this small universe, slowed down. The top row is the generation we're reading; the bottom row is the one being written. When two different numbers land on the same cell, a norm invents a replacement out of the empty space around it — measuring how far you must walk left and right in the row above to reach the nearest genes. Those two distances are v and u, and they appear as green calipers when it happens. A dotted line always joins the cell just written to the cell directly above it: that is the cell the rule consults, both to settle a collision and to decide where the gene copies next.

Field notes

Every cell holds a signed integer — a "gene" — or nothing at all. Each generation is written fresh from the one above it. A gene at position a holding value n writes a copy of itself one row down at position a+n: the sign sets direction, the magnitude sets distance. It does not stay where it was. For that reason, a cell’s "lineage" will appear as a diagonal.

Then the part Barricelli cared about: the gene now looks at the cell it landed on — as that cell stood in the row above — and if something is there, it copies itself again, traveling this time by that cell’s value instead of its own, up to the "copies per gene" limit. Every one of those copies sets out afresh from the gene’s original position, so a gene with other genes nearby scatters a small fan of offspring rather than continuing on in a line. The result is that a lone number can only ever step by itself, but a number with others nearby can be carried to places it could never reach alone. This is the dependency behind what Barricelli called a symbioorganism — a cooperating run of genes. To see why this is important, try setting "copies per gene" (in the "Universe" panel) to zero: a universe of several hundred slides steadily downward — a few dozen left by generation fifty, a handful by generation one hundred — and never recovers.

That dependency is also what lets the simulation develop organisms. A gene only earns its extra copies by reading some neighbor’s value, and where A reads B and B reads A, the two need each other — symbiosis. Where A reads B but B never reads A, A gains and B gains nothing — parasitism. A connected run of mutual readers is one organism; the ledger counts the symbiont cells and the organisms they form separately, and the show switch under the record dims everything else so you can see where each kind lives. They tend to hold different ground rather than mixing evenly — symbionts gather into coherent bands, parasites fill the spaces between.

But there’s more. What should happen when two different values land on the same cell? That’s where norms come in. The simplest norm (norm zero) is to have that cell become empty — no offspring is created. Four other norms (named A, B, C and D) create a new value for that cell, based on the cell at the same position above it in the previous generation. If something already occupies it there is no room to measure, and most norms simply empty the cell. If it is empty, they measure the gaps u and v out to the nearest occupied cells right and left in the row above, and build a number from those two distances. (An explanation of each norm can be found below or to the right of this text.)

Norms A and B each calculate a new value based on the sum of distances from an empty cell to the nearest cells left and right of it, and that has an important consequence. Every empty cell lying in the same gap measures the same u+v, because one distance grows exactly as fast as the other shrinks — between genes at L and R, any cell in between gives u+v = R−L. So norms A and B paint an entire gap one flat value. Norm C, on the other hand, subtracts the gaps instead of adding them, which results in a gradient across it. That is where the broad coherent fields of color in the record come from.

Barricelli ran different norms in different regions of the same universe, so that organisms drifting across a boundary met different rules and had to keep adapting; the four quarter selectors reproduce that, and the strip above the record marks where the boundaries fall. You can change which norm applies to which quadrant in the "Norms by region" panel.

A
New value is u+v — the two gaps added — kept positive if the flanking genes share a sign, negated if they disagree. A collision landing under an occupied cell empties instead.
B
As A, but u+v−1. A single unit of difference that changes the character of the whole field.
C
v−u: the difference of the gaps rather than their sum, so the value reports which side is closer.
D
A mirror test, and the only norm that ignores the gaps. With p the value directly above, compare the two cells p steps to either side; if both hold the same value m, the cell becomes 2m−p, otherwise it empties.
0
Every collision simply empties the cell — the plainest norm, and the one that kills the most.

The norms

When two different numbers land on the same cell, a norm decides what ends up there. None of them simply pick a winner — they invent a new value out of the local neighborhood in the row above. u is the distance rightward from the cell directly above the collision to the nearest gene; v is the distance leftward.

Barricelli ran different norms in different regions of the same universe, so that organisms drifting across a boundary met different rules and had to keep adapting. The four quarter selectors set which norm governs which quarter.
